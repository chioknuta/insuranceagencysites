Research / September 2026

We checked 2,735 insurance agency website addresses. Here is what owners should check on theirs.

By the Insurance Agency Sites team | Published September 29, 2026

Your agency’s website can load perfectly and still leave a visitor wondering what to do next. It can also fail in ways that are easy to miss when everyone in the office already knows the phone number.

We wanted to understand how often those problems appeared in our own prospect lists. On September 8, 2026, we ran an automated scan covering 2,735 website-address records associated with insurance agencies in Florida and Texas. We corrected the classifications on September 10 and separately rechecked the addresses that initially failed.

This was a snapshot of our lists, not a representative survey of every insurance agency. The findings offer a useful starting point for a practical website check.

What the scan found

Of the 2,735 records, 2,385 returned a response the scanner could process. Of those, 93 were classified as parking pages, placeholders or other responses that were not functioning agency websites. The remaining 2,292 records were classified as live sites. Some records share a domain; these are record-level counts.

Among those 2,292 records classified as live sites:

Homepage check Result
No HTML form detected 46.5%
No quote-request wording matching the scanner’s patterns 32.2%
No meta description detected 24.0%

A missing homepage form does not mean there is no way to request a quote. A form may be on another page, inside an embedded service, or loaded with JavaScript. Likewise, wording outside the scanner’s patterns can still provide a clear invitation to contact the agency. These are prompts for human review, not verdicts on a business.

An unsuccessful scan is not proof of a broken website

The first scan could not retrieve 350 recorded addresses successfully. It would be misleading to call all 350 broken.

Follow-up checks classified 197 as blocking automated access, and another 24 loaded on retry. Among the rest, 53 returned HTTP errors, 37 failed to resolve in DNS, and 29 resolved but did not accept a connection during the checks. Five showed parking pages, and five needed a manual redirect review.

These results describe the recorded addresses at the time of testing. They do not prove an agency had no website elsewhere, that a problem persisted, or that a customer lost access. An old domain in a contact list can also explain a failure.

The practical lesson is to open your actual website address yourself before drawing conclusions from a monitoring report.

Five checks worth doing this week

1. Open the website from your public profiles. Use the link on your business listing, social profile and email signature. Check on a phone as well as a computer. Each should lead to the current agency website without an error or parking page.

2. Follow the path a new customer would take. Can someone quickly see who you help and how to contact you? If trucking is a specialty, make that easy to discover. A clear phone number and a short request form can serve visitors with different preferences.

3. Test delivery, not just the appearance of the form. Send a clearly labeled test through your own form and confirm it reaches the person responsible for responding. Check the confirmation message and reply address too. Our scan did not submit forms or test inbox delivery.

4. Give existing clients a clear service route. Useful options can include certificate-of-insurance requests, policy-change requests and links to carriers’ billing or claims pages. Label requests clearly: submitting a form should not imply that coverage has changed or that a certificate has been issued. Ask only for the information needed to route the request, and use an appropriate secure channel for sensitive documents.

5. Decide who handles the next step. A website request needs an owner. Make it clear internally who checks the inbox, who covers absences and what happens to requests received after hours. A useful form and a reliable response process belong together.

Fix the specific problem before deciding to rebuild

A broken link or an out-of-date address may need a small correction. A website that no longer represents the agency, cannot be maintained, or makes routine requests difficult may need more work.

Start with evidence. Ask your provider to show you the problem, explain the fix and test the result. You should not need to commit to a replacement website just to find out what is wrong.

About this research

The source was an internal set of Florida and Texas insurance-agency prospect lists, not a random sample. Counts are per website-address record in the agency lists, not per unique domain. Some agencies share a website, so the figures must not be read as 2,735 distinct websites. The automated scan examined retrieved homepage HTML; it was not a full browser-based accessibility, usability, security or SEO audit. Some dynamic or embedded features may not have been detected. The September 10 correction separated non-site responses from live sites and fixed false matches in legacy-software detection. This article uses the corrected results. We did not measure sales, conversion rates, customer losses or the duration of outages. Findings are historical and individual websites may have changed.
